I Never Intended to Build an App Used Around the World
---
2026
---
How a small Royal Enfield Himalayan 450 dashboard experiment became OpenDash, an app used by riders around the world.
---

Sometimes projects begin because you have an idea.

Sometimes they begin because something quietly bothers you for long enough.

OpenDash belonged to the second category.

Like most riders, I was perfectly content with my Royal Enfield Himalayan 450. The motorcycle never felt incomplete. The dashboard, however, always did.

It could do navigation.

It could show ride information.

But every time I looked at that screen, I kept wondering—

*"Surely it can do more than this."*

That question stayed with me far longer than I expected.

## The rabbit hole began with another project

One evening I came across a Reddit post about **Northstar**.

It was an open-source project that streamed navigation from a phone directly to the Tripper Dash over Wi-Fi. Watching it work felt like discovering a hidden door inside a motorcycle I already owned.

Naturally, my designer brain began asking questions.

What if it could display photographs?

What if riders could personalise their dashboards?

What if the interface felt less like an engineering prototype and more like something people would actually enjoy looking at every day?

I reached out to the developer hoping some of those ideas were already planned.

After waiting for a while, I realised they probably weren't.

So I did what most curious people eventually end up doing.

I forked the project.

At first it was only meant to be a redesign.

Then another feature appeared.

Then another.

Eventually I looked at the repository and realised...

This wasn't really Northstar anymore.

The direction had become entirely my own.

So I started again.

From zero.

That became OpenDash.

---

## The first feature wasn't meant for riders

Ironically, the feature most people know OpenDash for today wasn't even built for motorcycle customisation.

It started because I was driving Rapido.

After every ride, passengers needed to scan my Google Pay QR code.

I wondered...

*"Wouldn't it be easier if I could simply display the QR code on my motorcycle dashboard?"*

So I tried.

It almost worked.

The Tripper Dash cropped the image.

Half the QR code disappeared.

Nobody could scan it.

A small problem.

But sometimes the smallest problems teach you the most.

Instead of cropping images, I wrote a **Fit Height** mode that preserved the entire image inside the display.

The QR code worked.

Then photographs worked.

Then wallpapers worked.

Looking back, it's funny.

The feature born out of a payment problem eventually became the soul of OpenDash.

---

## The project solved another frustration I had never spoken about

I've always disliked applications that pretend your own data belongs to them.

Fuel logs.

Maintenance history.

Expenses.

Travel records.

Every application wanted me to subscribe.

Almost none wanted me to leave.

No proper exports.

No imports.

No ownership.

That never sat right with me.

If someone records years of memories inside an application...

Those memories should remain theirs.

That philosophy quietly found its way into OpenDash.

I didn't want riders to feel trapped.

I wanted them to feel like they owned their information.

---

## I wasn't supposed to be writing code

The funny part?

I'm not a software engineer.

I'm a UI/UX designer.

I think in wireframes.

User journeys.

Interfaces.

Products.

Not backend architecture.

Most of the code came together with the help of ChatGPT.

People assume that made development easy.

It didn't.

Writing code was often the easy part.

Understanding why something broke at two in the morning...

That was the real challenge.

Every bug felt like another language I needed to learn.

Some nights the application barely opened.

Other nights one small change would unexpectedly fix three unrelated problems.

Somehow...

it all slowly came together.

---

## The idea that changed everything wasn't mine

There's one feature people associate with OpenDash more than anything else.

Wallpapers.

Ironically...

That wasn't my idea.

It was my father's.

He simply said,

*"People love their motorcycles. Let them make it feel like theirs."*

That's all.

One sentence.

Today, whenever I see riders placing photographs of their parents...

their pets...

their favourite rides...

or even their partners...

on the dashboard...

I realise he was right.

It stopped becoming software.

It became personal.

---

## The moment I realised strangers cared

One memory still makes me smile.

It was World Motorcycle Day.

I'd created a special wallpaper wishing everyone a Happy Motorcycle Day.

Nothing elaborate.

Just something fun.

At a traffic signal, I noticed another rider staring through my mirrors.

He kept leaning forward.

Trying to understand what he was looking at.

When the signal stayed red, I showed him the dashboard.

Without missing a beat he smiled and asked,

*"How did you do that?"*

When I told him it was my own application...

his reaction reminded me why building things is so addictive.

You never really know which small idea is going to make someone else's day.

---

## Then things became... strange

Initially only friends downloaded OpenDash.

Then their friends did.

Then motorcycle groups.

Then Instagram pages.

I uploaded one APK to GitHub.

Within roughly thirty-six hours it had spread across Himalayan 450 and Guerrilla 450 communities throughout India.

Friends started sending me reels.

Some were already using OpenDash without realising I had built it.

Then messages started arriving from Brazil.

Germany.

Portugal.

Australia.

People asked whether there would be an iPhone version.

Whether it would come to the Play Store.

Whether it supported their motorcycle.

That was surreal.

A project that began because I wanted a better dashboard...

was suddenly travelling much farther than I ever had.

---

## The lesson I learnt the hard way

There is one thing I wish I had handled better.

As OpenDash became more popular, I noticed many people had forgotten where the journey actually began.

Northstar deserved recognition.

Without it, OpenDash probably wouldn't exist.

Although both projects eventually became very different, inspiration deserves acknowledgment.

So I made sure to credit the original developer publicly.

Ideas grow because someone else plants the first seed.

There's nothing lost in admitting that.

---

## A phone call I'll never forget

There were many moments that made OpenDash feel real.

Friends telling me,

*"You're everywhere."*

People recognising the app at ride meets.

Watching customised dashboards appear on my Instagram feed.

But one moment quietly stood above everything else.

Royal Enfield reached out.

Not to complain.

To appreciate.

They even sent me gift hampers recognising the work behind the project.

That was probably the first time I stopped thinking,

*"Maybe this is just a side project."*

And started believing...

*"Maybe I'm capable of building things people genuinely care about."*

---

## This is only the first chapter

OpenDash gave me something much bigger than an application.

It gave me confidence.

Confidence that ideas don't need permission.

That you don't need to come from Computer Science to build software.

That good design can be just as important as good engineering.

Most importantly...

it reminded me that you don't have to build something for everyone.

You only have to solve one problem honestly.

If enough people share that problem...

they'll eventually find you.

And perhaps that's my biggest takeaway from this entire journey.

Keep building.

Keep learning.

Keep sharing your work.

You never really know which small idea will quietly change your life.
